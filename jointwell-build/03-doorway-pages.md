### Article

If your GP has said "wear and tear", you already know the shape of the day. The first steps out of bed are the worst. By mid-morning the knee has loosened. By evening it is complaining again, and the stairs are a decision rather than a habit.

This article is about the first part of that: the cold hour before the day starts. It is the part nobody treats, and it is the part that decides how the rest of the day goes.

**Why mornings are the worst**

Overnight a joint sits still. The fluid that cushions it settles, the muscles around it cool, and the stiffness you feel at 7am is not the arthritis getting worse. It is a joint that has not moved for eight hours. That is why the first ten minutes are the hardest and why the knee is often better once you have been to the kettle and back twice.

**What the NHS says about heat**

The NHS advises that heat applied to the affected area can be very effective in reducing pain, and lists warmth as one of the simple things to try for joint stiffness. Physiotherapists say the same: warm the joint, then move it. Heat before movement, ice after a flare.

Most people already do this. They have a wheat bag by the microwave. The problem is not the idea; it is the ten minutes. A wheat bag is hottest the moment you pick it up and cooler every minute after. By the time you have sat down with a cup of tea it is lukewarm, and back to the microwave you go. Nobody keeps that up every morning for a year.

A stiff shoulder is a strange thing to explain to people who have not had one. It does not stop you walking. It stops you doing up a bra strap, lifting the kettle with that arm, reaching the top shelf, and sleeping on that side. Small things, twenty times a day.

**Why a shoulder is hard to warm**

Every heat product you have tried was designed for a knee or a back. A wheat bag slides off a shoulder. A hot water bottle needs a hand to hold it, which is the hand you wanted to use. Rubs are messy and reach the skin, not the joint. So the shoulder stays cold, and cold joints are stiff joints.

Heat brings blood to the muscles around the joint and lets them relax. Massage does the same by a different route. Orthopaedic surgeons quoted on our homepage say heat therapy is an easy, inexpensive way to relieve some types of arthritis stiffness, and physiotherapists routinely warm a shoulder before working on it. - Keyword: heat pad after knee replacement UK
- Testimonial slot: `[PLACEHOLDER: verified review from someone who has had a knee replacement]`
- Paired ad: Angle 9d, After a knee replacement

### Article

First, the sentence that matters more than anything else on this page: ask your surgeon or physiotherapist before you put heat anywhere near a new knee. They know what was done, how the wound has healed and what they want the joint to do this week. What follows is general, and their word beats ours.

**Why people ask about heat**

After the swelling has settled and the wound has healed, most people are sent home with a sheet of exercises and told to do them several times a day. The exercises are the recovery. The trouble is that a stiff, cold knee does not want to bend, and the first few repetitions each morning are the ones people dread.

**What physios usually say**

In the early weeks, ice. The joint is swollen and ice is what brings that down. Later, once the wound is fully healed and your physio is happy, many people are told that warmth before exercises is fine and can help the muscles relax so the knee bends more easily. Ice again afterwards if it swells. This is the pattern, but only your own physio can say when you have moved from one stage to the next.

**How people use the wrap**

Once they have the go-ahead, the routine we hear about is: wrap on for twenty to thirty minutes with the morning tea, then the exercise sheet. The wrap holds a steady level from 45°C to 65°C rather than starting hot and fading, and the gentle massage setting is popular with people who find the muscles above the knee tight. It switches off by itself at thirty minutes. Start at the lowest level and only go up if your physio is happy.

**Never over the scar until it has healed**

If the wound is not fully closed, or your physio has said no, do not use heat. Heat on a healing wound or on an infected joint is a bad idea, and a wrap cannot tell the difference. If the knee is hot, red or more swollen than yesterday, that is a phone call to the clinic, not a heat wrap.

**What it is not**

The wrap does not speed up recovery and does not replace anything your surgical team gave you. It is comfort before the work, for people who have been told that is fine. If you have diabetes, circulation problems or reduced feeling in your skin, check with your GP before using any heat product.
