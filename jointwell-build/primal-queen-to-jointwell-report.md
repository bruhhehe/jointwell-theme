# From Primal Queen to Jointwell
## What a $5m-a-month supplement brand does on the page, and how to apply it to velagoods.co.uk for women aged 55 to 65+

Prepared 4 September 2026. Based on a full read of primalqueen.com and velagoods.co.uk on that date, plus third-party traffic and ad data.

---

## Part 1. The scale you are studying

Primal Queen (PQ) is not a clever landing page. It is an ad machine with a landing page bolted to the back. Third-party tracking puts it at roughly 2 million monthly visits, an estimated $3.5m to $6.4m in monthly revenue, and around 3,000 active Meta ads at once. The page you read is built to convert paid traffic that already arrived warm from an ad, and every decision on it follows from that.

Two things to hold in mind before copying anything:

1. PQ sells a consumable on subscription at $39 a month, with a 365-day guarantee. Their economics tolerate a high cost per customer because the customer keeps paying. You sell a £49.96 device once. You cannot spend like them, and you should not structure your offer like them without building a reason for repeat revenue (covered in Part 5).
2. PQ has attracted serious criticism: reviewers have called out a 675 mg daily dose that is roughly four times lower than comparable organ supplements, a proprietary blend that hides individual amounts, and marketing claims that do not hold up. Their page also runs claims that would not survive the UK ASA. Take the mechanics. Leave the hype.

## Part 2. What Primal Queen actually does, section by section

### 2.1 They sell a feeling first and a product fourth

Their H1 is not "Beef Organ Supplement". It is: **STOP SETTLING for low energy, hormonal chaos, and feeling like a stranger in your own body.**

The product name does not appear until the reader has already nodded. The hero speaks to a woman's Tuesday afternoon, not to a specification.

**Jointwell today:** your H1 is "Jointwell Heated Joint Massager". That is the name of the thing. Your best line on the entire page is buried halfway down: *"Get down on the floor with the kids. And get back up again."* That should be at the top.

### 2.2 They name an enemy

"How our ancestors THRIVED before we trusted big corporations more than our own bodies." "They're focused on keeping you hooked on quick fixes." A villain gives the reader someone to side against, and siding against something is easier than believing in something.

**Jointwell today:** you have a villain and you handle it well: the wheat bag, the pills that wear off, the £50 physio. Your line "None of them failed because your joints are too far gone" is stronger than anything on PQ because it forgives the reader. Keep it. Move it higher.

### 2.3 They give the customer an identity

"Queen." "Unleash your inner Primal Queen." "An army of thousands of Queens that have taken back their health." "Sorry guys!" Customers are not buying capsules; they are joining a tribe with a name. Every review is framed as "Here's what Primal Queens are saying."

**Jointwell today:** no identity, no "we", no group to join. The customer is alone with a wrap.

### 2.4 The founder is a person with a face and a before story

"Hey, I'm Shelby." Photo. A list of what she tried (low carb, paleo, greens, keto, Zumba) and a list of what she suffered (weight gain, inflammation, low energy). It ends: "That's when the Primal Queen movement was born."

Nobody checks whether Shelby's story is true. It works because it converts a faceless company into a woman who had the same problem.

**Jointwell today:** no name, no face, no origin. Your own FAQ asks "How do I know this isn't another Facebook gadget?" and answers with logistics. The honest answer a 60-year-old woman wants is: *who are you?* Anonymous plus an unfamiliar brand (Jointwell) on a mismatched domain (velagoods) plus 4 to 8 day delivery reads as dropshipping to her, however honest your copy is.

### 2.5 The same CTA block appears after every section

"UNLOCK YOUR TRUE POWER" + "365-Day Money Back Guarantee" + "Free Shipping" appears roughly nine times. A scrolling marquee of the offer appears three times. A sticky bar follows the reader down the page. They assume the reader might convert at any scroll depth and never make her scroll back up to find the button.

**Jointwell today:** one CTA in the hero, one near the guarantee, one at the bottom, plus a sticky bar. Good foundation, too sparse. The guarantee is only stated in full once.

### 2.6 The offer is itemised with a price next to every free thing

```
2 Month Supply (2 bags)          $132  →  $78
Free 3rd bag on first order      $39
Free bamboo lid glass jar        $24
Free on-the-go pill case         $9
Free mystery gift                $9
Free USA shipping                $12
```

Every free item carries a dollar figure. The reader adds them up and the £ she pays looks small against the stack. The 3-month plan is preselected and shown first.

**Jointwell today:** you have tiers (1/2/4 wraps) with strike-through anchors and savings called out. Good. But the free items (extension strap, USB-C cable, mugwort sachet, free tracked delivery, 2-year warranty, free return postage) are scattered across the page with no value attached. That is a stack you already own and have not priced.

### 2.7 A timeline of what to expect, and when

1 week: energy and mood. 1 month: vitality. 2 months: less irritability. 3 months: skin. 6 months: mental clarity. 12 months: hormones.

This does three jobs at once: it sets expectations so early refund requests drop, it justifies the subscription ("you need 6 months"), and it makes the reader picture herself using the product for a year.

**Jointwell today:** no timeline. A woman with a ten-year knee does not know whether to expect anything on day one or in week three. Your 30-day trial would convert better if the page told her what day 1, day 7 and day 30 feel like.

### 2.8 One product, seven doorways

"See what Primal Queen can do for you: Weight Loss / Hormones / Hysterectomy / Iron Deficiency / Low Energy / Perimenopause / Menopause." Same capsule, seven entry points, each matching an ad angle. A woman who clicked a menopause ad lands and sees her word.

**Jointwell today:** three joints (knee, shoulder, elbow) and one generic condition list. No doorway for "knee arthritis", "frozen shoulder", "tennis elbow from gardening", "after a knee replacement", "stiff first thing in the morning", "can't kneel any more".

### 2.9 Social proof with faces, names, numbers and screenshots

"1M+ Happy Customers. 4.64/5." Five named women with photos at the top. A block of screenshotted reviews (Crystal Weber, Lindsey West) that look like Facebook comments. A Stamped review widget in the nav.

Note the 4.64, not 5.0. A perfect score reads as fake; 4.6 reads as real.

**Jointwell today:** you have a "What real buyers say" section with three named customers (Maggie 64, Sue 68, Janet 70), photos in their own kitchens and garden, five stars, a verified tick and quotes that read like speech ("by the time I've finished the cup I can get down the stairs properly"). This is the right register for the audience and better written than PQ's reviews. Two things hold it back:

- **Placement.** It sits well below the buy box. PQ puts five faces beside the headline. Move one card (Maggie, since she is holding the product) up next to the price, keep the three-card section where it is, and change the top star line from "Trusted UK seller" to a count and average pulled from Judge.me ("4.8 from 41 verified reviews").
- **The photos need to look as real as the words.** Three polished, evenly lit portraits in the same pose can read as stock or AI to a sceptical 60-year-old, and this is the most sceptical audience online. Use the customers' own phone photos if you have them, add "photo supplied by customer", and link each card to the underlying Judge.me review so the verified tick means something a reader can click. A slightly blurry photo of Sue in her armchair sells more than a perfect one because the imperfection is the proof.

### 2.10 Third-party proof, stated plainly

Link to a Eurofins lab PDF. Six "promise" icons. "FDA-registered facilities."

**Jointwell today:** CE certificate linked, and your section "We are the ones selling it. So don't take our word for any of it" is better than anything PQ writes. It is the most trust-building copy I have seen on a small UK store. Keep every word.

### 2.11 Urgency, some of it manufactured

Countdown timer. "September has a very high risk of selling out." "Ships today if ordered within [timer]."

**Do not copy the fake scarcity.** The UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act (in force for consumer protections since April 2025) bans fake urgency and fake reviews outright, with fines up to 10% of turnover. Your launch-price deadline of 8 September is real, so it is fine. A "ships today if ordered by 2pm" timer is fine if true. "High risk of selling out" is not.

### 2.12 A product ladder behind the front door

Beef Organs → PQ7 (weight loss) → Goddess Bites (brain, muscle) → Marrow (joints) → Build Your Own Bundle. Each new SKU is a new ad angle and a new reason to email the list.

**Jointwell today:** one SKU. After she buys, you have nothing to sell her and no reason to email her.

## Part 3. Where Jointwell is already ahead

You should know what not to break.

- **Honesty as a device.** "Everything above this line was written by us. That makes all of it worth less than it should be." PQ could never write that. Your demographic, who have been burned by Facebook ads, will read it twice.
- **The comparison table.** Jointwell vs wheat bag vs creams vs TENS vs physio, with running costs. Concrete and fair.
- **The heat-decay chart.** Wheat bag fades, Jointwell holds. One visual that makes the whole case.
- **Return postage paid.** PQ makes you pay return shipping. You do not. Say it louder.
- **Clinician quotes plus NHS and Harvard.** Authority borrowed, not invented.
- **Real setting photography.** Kitchen table, jigsaw with a grandchild, crossword in the armchair. PQ uses stock-looking product shots. Yours are better for this audience.
- **Testimonials in the customer's own voice.** Maggie, Sue and Janet at 64, 68 and 70, with the tea, the telly and the daughter borrowing it. The ages alone do more work than PQ's "1M+ happy customers", because they tell the reader "women like me buy this."

## Part 4. Two things to fix before spending a pound on ads

### 4.1 "Clinically proven" has to go

Your hero says *"Clinically proven to ease pain, stiffness and improve blood flow in a single 30-minute session."* Your footer says *"It is not a medical treatment and does not cure, treat or prevent any disease."* Your trust section says the CE certificate "does not say this is a medical device, and we are not going to imply that it does."

The hero implies exactly that. Under the CAP Code the ASA will want evidence for "clinically proven" that relates to this device, not to heat therapy in general, and the MHRA may take a view on a non-medical device claiming to ease pain from arthritis.

Replace with borrowed authority you can substantiate: *"Heat and massage are what the NHS, Harvard Health and orthopaedic surgeons recommend for stiff joints. Jointwell puts both in one wrap you strap on at the kitchen table."* Same persuasive weight, no claim you have to defend.

### 4.2 Put the reviews where the money is, and make them checkable

You already have the three-card testimonial section. Now do four things with it:

1. Move one card beside the price and buy button. A reader deciding at the top should see a woman her age before she sees the tier picker.
2. Replace "★★★★★ Trusted UK seller" with a live count and average from Judge.me. A number she can click beats five stars she cannot.
3. Link each card to its Judge.me review and use the customer's own photo where you have one, captioned "photo supplied by customer". Your trust section promises that reviews are verified and unedited; the cards should visibly honour that promise, because fake or unverifiable reviews are banned under the DMCC Act and this audience checks.
4. Keep the pipeline running: review request at day 10 after delivery, a second at day 25 tied to the guarantee ("before your 30 days are up, tell us honestly"), any incentive offered for all reviews rather than positive ones. Aim for 50 verified reviews by day 60 so the count at the top keeps climbing.

## Part 5. The rebuild, principle by principle

### 5.1 Positioning: pick the woman and speak only to her

PQ says "sorry guys" and grows faster because of it. Your photos include a man at the kitchen table and a man doing the crossword. Your demographic is women 55 to 65+. Choose.

Your woman: 58 to 68, knees or a shoulder that have got worse over five years, two or three things in the cupboard that half work, a GP who says "wear and tear", grandchildren she wants to get on the floor with, a garden, a dog walk she has shortened. She is on Facebook daily. She does not trust adverts and she does trust other women her age.

Everything on the page should be written to her. Men will still buy. Women buy for men.

### 5.2 Identity: give her a name for what she is doing

PQ's customers are Queens. Yours need something. Not a cute brand word she would be embarrassed by. A phrase she would say herself.

Working options: *"the morning half hour"*, *"the kettle club"*, *"getting back down on the floor"*. Build the identity around a ritual rather than a title: the wrap goes on with the first cup of tea. Every email, every ad and every review request references the morning half hour. Customers who share a ritual become a group.

### 5.3 Founder: put a name and a face on it

Write the Jointwell equivalent of "Hey, I'm Shelby." It must be true. Whoever runs Vela Goods, or a parent or relative whose knee started this, goes on the page with a photo taken in a real kitchen.

Structure it as PQ does:
- what she (or you, or your mum) tried: the wheat bag, Deep Heat, cocodamol, three physio visits at £50, the TENS machine now in a drawer
- what none of it did
- why this wrap, and why the return postage is paid ("because I sent enough gadgets back myself")

Three hundred words. It answers "who are you" better than any logistics FAQ.

Also resolve the brand/domain split. Either move to jointwell.co.uk (redirect the old domain) or put "Jointwell by Vela Goods, [town], UK" in the header and footer with a UK address. An unfamiliar name on a different unfamiliar domain costs you trust with every visitor.

### 5.4 Headline: feeling first

Current: *Jointwell Heated Joint Massager*

Candidates, in PQ's structure (stop enduring the symptom → picture the outcome):

- *Stop planning your day around one knee.*
- *Get down on the floor with the grandchildren. And get back up again.*
- *Thirty minutes of warmth that doesn't go cold, every morning, on the joint that runs your day.*
- *Your wheat bag lasts ten minutes. Your knee hurts all day.*

Sub-line: *A cordless heated wrap with massage for knees, shoulders and elbows. Made for arthritis, worn joints and long-term stiffness. Try it for 30 days on your worst joint. If it doesn't help, we pay the postage back.*

Then the product name.

### 5.5 Offer: itemise the stack and put a value on every line

```
Jointwell heated joint wrap                       £62.45   £49.96
Extension strap (shoulder and elbow)              £9        Free
USB-C cable                                       £6        Free
Mugwort warming sachet                            £4        Free
Tracked UK delivery                               £4.95     Free
30-day trial, return postage paid                 —         Free
2-year warranty, UK support                       —         Included
Total value £86.40. You pay £49.96.
```

Add one physical bonus you can source for under £3 that raises perceived value by £10 to £15: a printed "30-day morning routine" card, a spare non-slip inner liner, or a second sachet pack. PQ's "mystery gift" costs them almost nothing and appears on the stack at $9.

Keep the three tiers. Reorder so "Two wraps" is preselected (it already carries "Most chosen") and rename the framing: *One for each knee* / *One for you, one for your sister*.

### 5.6 Guarantee: 30 days is short for this audience

PQ runs 365 days on a consumable. You are on 30 for a device with a 2-year warranty. Move to 60 days minimum, ideally 90, and name it: *"The 90-day worst-joint trial."* Returns on heat wraps are low; the extra 60 days costs you little and removes the "what if it takes me a while to try it properly" objection that a 62-year-old actually has. Repeat the guarantee in short form under every CTA, as PQ does.

### 5.7 Expectation timeline

Add a section between the comparison table and the FAQ:

- **Day 1.** Warmth you set, not warmth you wait for. Most people go straight to level 3.
- **Day 3.** The morning half hour becomes a habit. Kettle on, wrap on.
- **Week 2.** This is when people tell us the first-thing-in-the-morning stiffness is easier to walk off.
- **Week 4.** Your 30 days are up. You decide. Keep it or post it back on us.
- **Month 3 and beyond.** Still charging on USB-C, still on your knee at breakfast. The warranty runs two years.

Phrase everything as usage and habit, not as medical outcome. "People tell us" is a report, not a claim.

### 5.8 Doorways: one wrap, many conditions

Build the "See what Jointwell can do for you" strip: **Knee arthritis / Stiff shoulder / Tennis elbow / After a knee replacement / Morning stiffness / Kneeling and gardening / Cold weather aches.** Each links to a short section or journal post with one matching review and the same buy button. Each becomes an ad angle. Your Journal exists; this is what it is for.

### 5.9 Repetition

After every major section: CTA button + three-line trust strip (*90-day trial, we pay return postage · 2-year UK warranty · Free tracked delivery*). Eight to nine times on the page. It feels excessive when you build it and invisible when she reads it.

### 5.10 The product ladder (this is where PQ's model actually pays)

You have no second purchase. Build one within 90 days:

1. **Second wrap** (already in the tiers; email every single-wrap buyer at day 20 with a partner price).
2. **Replacement strap and liner pack**, £9.95. Small margin, high frequency, keeps them opening your emails.
3. **Mugwort sachet refills**, 6-pack £8.95. Your consumable. PQ's whole business is a consumable.
4. **Neck-and-shoulder version**, then **lower-back version**. Same supplier category, new ad angle, new email to the existing list.
5. **Gift bundle** in November (two wraps, gift box, card). Women 55 to 65 buy for sisters, husbands and their own mothers.

With a £9 refill and a second-wrap upsell you take average customer value from £50 toward £75 to £90, which is the difference between Meta ads being unprofitable and profitable.

## Part 6. Acquisition: where PQ spends and where you should

PQ runs thousands of Meta ads. You do not need thousands; you need ten concepts tested properly on Facebook (not Instagram: your woman is on Facebook), targeting women 55 to 70 in the UK, broad within that.

**Ad angles, each mapped to a page doorway:**

1. *The wheat bag confession.* "I've microwaved this thing four times tonight." UGC-style video from a woman in a real kitchen.
2. *Founder story.* The 300-word origin, read to camera, subtitled.
3. *Side-by-side heat test.* Hot water bottle vs Jointwell at 0, 15 and 30 minutes with a kitchen thermometer. Your best proof, and it costs nothing to film.
4. *The floor.* Grandmother, jigsaw, carpet, gets up. No voiceover needed.
5. *"Not another Facebook gadget."* Lead with the objection. Show the UK returns address, the paid return postage, the Judge.me reviews.
6. *Comparison table as a static image.* Your existing table with the running costs.
7. *Physio's £50.* "What the physio does with her hands for £50 a visit, done in your armchair for £49.96 once."
8. *Review carousel.* Maggie, Sue and Janet as three static cards with their quotes and ages, one button. You already have the asset; this should be one of the first four ads you test.
9. *Condition-specific.* Knee arthritis version, shoulder version, elbow-from-gardening version.
10. *Guarantee-only.* "Try it on your worst knee for 90 days. If it doesn't help, we pay the postage back." Nothing else.

**Format notes for 55 to 65:** large subtitles, slower cuts, one idea per ad, real homes, no music-video editing, no models under fifty, and text she can read without her glasses. PQ's ads are loud; yours should be calm and specific.

**Budget realism:** at £49.96 with a single-SKU store, break-even on Meta needs a cost per purchase under about £20 to £25. That is hard without a founder, an upsell, and reviews placed where cold traffic sees them. Do Parts 4 and 5 first, then spend £30 to £50 a day for three weeks testing angles 1, 3, 5 and 10 before scaling anything.

**Organic that PQ uses and you can afford:**
- A Facebook Group for customers ("the morning half hour"). Weekly post asking what the wrap went on today. Screenshot the best replies for the page, with permission.
- Journal posts for each doorway (Part 5.8), written plainly, 600 to 900 words, ranking for "heated knee wrap UK", "heat or ice for arthritic knee", "best heat pad for shoulder arthritis".
- Email flows: welcome (founder story), day 3 (how to get the most out of it), day 10 (review request), day 20 (second wrap for a partner), day 45 (refill sachets), monthly journal digest.

## Part 7. What not to copy from Primal Queen

| PQ tactic | Why you leave it |
|---|---|
| "Ancient secret", "unleash your true power" | Your woman finds it silly. Your existing voice is your advantage. |
| "High risk of selling out" | Fake scarcity is now illegal in the UK. |
| Proprietary blend hiding the dose | Your equivalent would be vague specs. Keep the 45 to 65°C, 360 g, 46 cm figures. Specifics are your trust. |
| Perfect-looking testimonials without verification | You already committed to Judge.me verified reviews. Show them and say so. |
| "Sorry guys" tone | Take the principle (choose the woman), not the phrasing. |
| Subscription default with a pre-ticked box | Not for a device. Build repeat revenue through refills and second products instead. |

## Part 8. The 90-day sequence

**Weeks 1 to 2: foundations.**
Remove "clinically proven". Move a testimonial card beside the price, swap the top star line for a live Judge.me count, link each card to its verified review. Launch review-collection emails. Write and photograph the founder section. Resolve the domain/brand mismatch. Add the itemised offer stack. Extend the guarantee to 60 or 90 days.

**Weeks 3 to 4: page rebuild.**
New H1 and sub-line. Repeated CTA and trust strip after every section. Expectation timeline. Condition doorway strip. Move "That's not on you" above the fold. Preselect the two-wrap tier.

**Weeks 5 to 8: proof and ladder.**
Film the heat-test video and the founder video on a phone. Open the Facebook Group and seed it with existing customers. Source the refill sachets and strap pack. Publish four journal doorway posts.

**Weeks 9 to 12: paid.**
Test four ad angles at £30 to £50 a day on Facebook, women 55 to 70, UK. Kill anything above £30 cost per purchase after 20 purchases. Scale the winner. Launch the day-20 second-wrap email and the day-45 refill email to everyone who has bought.

**Metrics to watch:** landing page conversion (target 2.5 to 4% on warm Facebook traffic once reviews are live), cost per purchase (under £25), average order value (target £70 with two-wrap preselect and stack), review count (50 by day 60), refund rate (under 5%).

## Summary in one paragraph

Primal Queen wins by leading with how a woman feels, giving her a founder to trust and a tribe to join, itemising an offer so the price looks small, repeating the guarantee until it is background noise, telling her what to expect by week, opening one product through seven condition doorways, and building a ladder of second purchases. Jointwell already has better honesty, better proof, better photography and better-written testimonials than PQ. It is missing the founder's face, the identity, the stack, the timeline, the doorways and the second product, and the reviews it has sit too far from the buy button. Fix the "clinically proven" line and move the reviews up first. Then rebuild the page in the order above, and only then spend on ads.
